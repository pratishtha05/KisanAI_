This document covers cross-crop irrigation principles. For crop-specific critical growth
stages and intervals, see knowledge_base/crops/ — use this document for method selection,
scheduling logic, and water-saving practices that apply across crops.

## Irrigation Methods Compared

| Method | Water Use Efficiency | Best Suited For | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flood / Basin | Low (40-50%) | Rice (which needs standing water anyway), flat fields with assured water | Highest water loss to seepage/runoff; simplest, lowest upfront cost |
| Furrow | Medium (50-60%) | Row crops — cotton, sugarcane, maize | Water flows along furrows between raised beds; moderate efficiency |
| Sprinkler | Medium-high (65-75%) | Wheat, groundnut, vegetables on undulating land | Good for light, frequent irrigation; upfront equipment cost |
| Drip | High (85-95%) | Fruit orchards, cotton, sugarcane, vegetables | Highest efficiency, precise delivery to root zone, highest upfront cost but strong long-term water/labor savings |

Method choice is a real trade-off between water efficiency and upfront cost — a
cash-constrained smallholder on flood irrigation isn't necessarily making a "wrong"
choice; drip/sprinkler subsidies (many state governments offer 50-90% subsidy on
drip installation) are worth surfacing specifically because the efficiency gain matters
most exactly where water is scarcest.

## The General Principle Behind "Critical Growth Stages"

Every crop guide in this knowledge base lists specific critical stages (e.g., wheat's
Crown Root Initiation, rice's panicle initiation, maize's silking). The general pattern
behind all of them: a crop is most water-sensitive during (a) establishment/germination,
and (b) reproductive stages (flowering, grain/fruit filling) — vegetative-stage water
stress reduces yield somewhat, but reproductive-stage water stress can cause outright
crop failure for that season (flower/fruit abortion). When water is limited, prioritizing
irrigation at reproductive stages over vegetative stages is the general triage principle.

## Signs of Under- and Over-Irrigation

**Under-irrigation signs**: leaf rolling/curling (especially visible in cereals at midday),
wilting that doesn't recover by evening, stunted growth, premature flowering (a stress
response), delayed/patchy germination.

**Over-irrigation / waterlogging signs**: yellowing from the base of the plant upward
(root oxygen deprivation, not a nutrient issue — easy to misdiagnose as N deficiency),
stunted root growth, increased disease incidence (most fungal root rots thrive in
waterlogged soil), standing water visible more than a day after irrigation on
non-paddy crops.

## Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD) — Rice-Specific Water Saving

Covered in more detail in crops/rice.md, but worth flagging as the single most
impactful water-saving technique available for rice specifically: allowing the field
to dry until water level drops ~15cm below the surface before re-irrigating, rather
than maintaining continuous standing water, cuts water use 15-30% with minimal yield
impact, and also reduces methane emissions and brown planthopper pressure. Not
applicable to non-paddy crops, which shouldn't have standing water in the first place.

## Rainfall Integration

An irrigation recommendation should always account for recent and forecast rainfall,
not just "days since last irrigation":
- Skip a scheduled irrigation if >20mm rain fell in the preceding 3-5 days and the
  field isn't showing under-irrigation signs.
- A forecast of significant rain in the next 24-48 hours is a reason to delay a
  non-urgent irrigation, both to save water/cost and to avoid over-saturating the
  field right before rainfall adds more.
- This is the kind of check a Weather Intelligence module should feed directly into
  irrigation timing recommendations — irrigation advice generated without checking
  the forecast risks recommending unnecessary watering right before rain.
